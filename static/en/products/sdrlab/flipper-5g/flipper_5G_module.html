Introduction & Overview

What this board does and what you'll be able to accomplish

Welcome to the SDRLab Flipper Zero 5G Add-On Board user manual. This guide is written for beginners — you don't need a deep technical background to follow along. By the end, you'll be able to set up the board, scan Wi-Fi networks on both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz, and understand the key security research tools it provides.

What Is This Board?

The Flipper Zero 5G Add-On Board is a hardware expansion module that plugs directly onto your Flipper Zero device via its GPIO pins. It adds a dedicated dual-band Wi-Fi chip (Realtek RTL8720DN) with BLE 5.0 — turning your Flipper Zero into a powerful wireless security research platform.

ℹ️
What is Flipper Zero?

Flipper Zero is a portable multi-tool for security researchers and hobbyists. It supports RFID, NFC, Sub-GHz radio, Infrared, and GPIO expansion — all in a dolphin-shaped device. Learn more at flipper.net

What You'll Be Able to Do

  • Scan 5 GHz Wi-Fi networks — previously inaccessible with older Flipper add-ons
  • Run Wi-Fi deauthentication tests on your own authorized networks
  • Map nearby wireless environments across both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands
  • Prototype Evil Portal captive portal scenarios for phishing-awareness testing
  • Perform beacon flood tests to study RF congestion
  • Scan Bluetooth Low Energy (BLE 5.0) devices alongside Wi-Fi
  • Develop custom firmware using PlatformIO (advanced)
🔴
Legal Notice — Read First

All features of this board must only be used on networks you own or have explicit written permission to test. Using Wi-Fi deauthentication, Evil Portal, or beacon flood on unauthorized networks is illegal in most countries. See Chapter 10 for full legal guidance.

Before You Begin

Prerequisites, firmware requirements, and what you need

What You Need

  • 🐬
    Flipper Zero Device
    Original Flipper Zero (any color). The board connects via the standard GPIO header on the top of your Flipper Zero.
  • 📡
    This SDRLab 5G Add-On Board
    The RTL8720DN/BW16-based expansion board. Check which antenna variant you have: PCB trace (built-in) or U.FL connector (for external antenna).
  • ⚡
    Momentum OR Unleashed Custom Firmware
    Critical: Stock Flipper Zero firmware does NOT support this board. You must flash Momentum or Unleashed firmware first.
  • 🔌
    USB-C Cable (optional)
    Required only for firmware updates or if you plan to develop custom firmware with PlatformIO.
⚠️
Stock Firmware Won't Work

If your Flipper Zero is running the official firmware from flipper.net, this board will not function. You need to install Momentum or Unleashed firmware before proceeding. The WiFi Marauder app is only available in these community frameworks.

How to Check Your Current Firmware

1

Open Settings

From the Flipper Zero main menu, navigate to Settings using the directional pad.

2

Go to About

Navigate to About → the firmware version is displayed. Look for "Momentum" or "Unleashed" in the version string.

3

Check the Result

If you see "Official" or only a version number like "0.xx.x" — you are on stock firmware. If you see "Momentum vX.X" or "Unleashed vXX" — you're ready.

Installing Momentum Firmware (If Needed)

Momentum is the recommended firmware for this board. Follow these steps:

  1. Visit github.com/Next-Flip/Momentum-Firmware
  2. Download the latest release .tgz file
  3. Open the Flipper Mobile App on your phone → Connect your Flipper Zero via Bluetooth
  4. Go to Device → Firmware Update → select the downloaded file
  5. Wait for the update to complete and your Flipper to reboot
💡
Tip: Keep a Backup

Before changing firmware, back up your Flipper Zero's SD card data using qFlipper (the desktop app). Your RFID, NFC, and Sub-GHz saves are stored on the SD card and will survive a firmware change, but a backup is always a good habit.

Hardware Overview

Board layout, components, and quick-reference specifications

Key Components on the Board

  • Realtek RTL8720DN (AI Thinker BW16 module) — the main wireless chip. The small silver rectangular module on the board's surface is the BW16.
  • GPIO Header (2×8 pins) — the row of pins along one edge that connects to your Flipper Zero. The spacing matches Flipper's standard header exactly.
  • LED Indicator — a small LED near the chip. Lights up briefly on power-on; may blink during active scanning depending on firmware.
  • Antenna Connector (U.FL/IPEX) — a tiny coaxial connector for attaching an external antenna. Only present on U.FL revision boards. If your board has a trace antenna instead, no connector is visible.
  • PCB Trace Antenna — a zigzag copper pattern etched into the board (present on PCB-antenna revision). No external connector needed.

Board Revisions

Two hardware revisions exist. Check your board to identify which you have:

  • U.FL Revision — has a small IPEX/U.FL coaxial connector. You can attach an external antenna for extended range. Look for a tiny gold connector near the edge of the BW16 module.
  • PCB Antenna Revision — no connector visible. Uses an etched copper trace on the PCB as the antenna. More compact but range is limited to ~10–30m line-of-sight.

ℹ️
Why Two CPU Cores?

The Cortex-M4 at 200 MHz handles active work: processing Wi-Fi frames, running firmware logic, and communicating with Flipper Zero. The Cortex-M0 at 20 MHz handles background tasks efficiently — this is what allows the board to do passive monitoring without heavily draining your Flipper Zero's battery.

Physical Connection

How to correctly attach the board to your Flipper Zero

🔴
Power Off Before Connecting

Always power off your Flipper Zero before attaching or detaching this add-on board. Hot-plugging can damage the GPIO pins on both devices.

Step-by-Step Connection Guide

1

Power Off Your Flipper Zero

Hold the back button for 3 seconds → select Power Off. Wait until the screen goes fully dark.

2

Identify the GPIO Header

The GPIO header is the row of metal pins on the top edge of your Flipper Zero (the end away from the USB-C port). It has 2 rows of 8 pins each (16 pins total).

3

Orient the Board Correctly

The add-on board extends upward and outward from the Flipper's top edge. The antenna side of the board (or the BW16 module) should face away from the Flipper Zero screen. There is only one physically correct orientation — the pin header alignment prevents reverse insertion.

4

Press Firmly Onto the Header

Align the female socket on the add-on board with the Flipper Zero's GPIO pins. Press down evenly and firmly until the board is fully seated. You should feel a slight resistance, then a firm stop. Do not force it.

5

Power On and Verify

Press the power button on Flipper Zero to turn it on. The add-on board's LED may briefly blink during boot. The Flipper Zero screen should show the normal boot animation — if it freezes or shows an error, power off and re-seat the board.

Anti-Static Precautions

  • Avoid handling the board by its components — hold it by the PCB edges
  • Ground yourself before handling (touch a metal surface or wear an anti-static wrist strap)
  • Store the board in its anti-static bag when not in use
  • Keep away from static-prone surfaces (carpet, plastic bags)
⚠️
External Antenna (U.FL Revision Only)

If your board has a U.FL connector and you want to attach an external antenna: insert the U.FL connector with a straight downward press — do not angle it. Angled insertion can break the tiny connector. To remove, use a non-conductive pry tool (plastic spudger).

Your First 5G Scan

Finding and launching the Wi-Fi Marauder app for the first time

Step 1 — Configure GPIO Pin Settings

Before the board can communicate with Flipper Zero, you need to set the correct GPIO pin assignments in your firmware settings. This is a one-time setup:

1

Open Momentum Settings (Momentum firmware)

Main Menu → Momentum → Protocol Settings → GPIO Pin Settings → ESP32 Pin Change

2

Set Pins to 15 and 16

Set TX Pin = 15 and RX Pin = 16. These are the UART communication pins used by this board. Press OK to confirm each setting.

3

Return to Main Menu

Press the back button to return to the main menu. The settings are saved automatically.

ℹ️
For Unleashed Firmware Users

The menu path may vary slightly by version, but the GPIO pin setting (TX=15, RX=16) applies the same way. Look for Settings → Protocols → GPIO / ESP32 or similar.

Step 2 — Launch WiFi Marauder

1

Navigate to GPIO Apps

Main Menu → Apps → GPIO

2

Select WiFi Marauder

Find and select [ESP32] WiFi Marauder. If you don't see it, you need to install it via the Momentum app catalog (see note below).

3

App Initializes

The app will show a startup screen. If the board is correctly connected and pin settings are right, you'll see the Marauder main menu within 2–3 seconds. If it shows "Board not detected" — revisit Chapter 4 and the pin settings.

Step 3 — Run Your First Scan

1

Select "Scan APs"

In the Marauder menu, navigate to Scan → Scan APs (Access Points). This starts a passive scan on both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz bands.

2

Watch Networks Populate

Networks will start appearing as the board sweeps through channels. Each entry shows the SSID (name), channel, RSSI (signal strength), and encryption type.

3

Stop and Review

Press the OK button or back to stop the scan. You can now browse the list of discovered networks.

Reading Scan Results — Beginner's Guide

WiFi Marauder — AP Scan Output
# Format: ID | SSID | BSSID | CH | RSSI | Encryption 0 | MyHomeNetwork | AA:BB:CC:DD:EE:FF | 6 | -42 | WPA2 1 | OfficeWifi_5G | 11:22:33:44:55:66 | 36 | -61 | WPA3 2 | CafeGuest | AA:11:BB:22:CC:33 | 11 | -78 | WPA2 3 | Hidden Network | 77:88:99:AA:BB:CC | 149| -55 | WPA2 # CH 1-14 = 2.4 GHz | CH 36-165 = 5 GHz (this board's advantage!) # RSSI: -30 excellent | -60 good | -80 weak | -90 very weak
  • SSID — the human-readable network name (e.g. "MyHomeNetwork"). A blank or "<hidden>" entry means the network is broadcasting a hidden SSID.
  • BSSID — the MAC address of the access point (router/AP hardware address)
  • CH — the Wi-Fi channel. Channels 1–14 are 2.4 GHz; channels 36, 40, 44… 149, 153, 157, 161, 165 are 5 GHz.
  • RSSI — Received Signal Strength Indicator in dBm. Closer to 0 = stronger. −42 is excellent; −78 is acceptable; −90 is very weak.
  • Encryption — security protocol: WPA3 (best), WPA2 (common), WPA (old), WEP (legacy/insecure), OPEN (no password)

Core Features Deep Dive

How each major capability works and when to use it

6.1 — Wi-Fi Deauthentication
🔴
Authorized Networks Only

Deauthentication attacks sent on networks you do not own are illegal under computer fraud laws in most countries. Only perform this on your own home router or in explicitly authorized test environments. Violations carry serious criminal penalties. See Chapter 10.

What is Deauthentication?

In older Wi-Fi standards (pre-WPA3), there is a management frame called a deauthentication frame. This frame tells a connected device "you are no longer authenticated to this network — disconnect immediately." The problem: these frames are unauthenticated — any device can send them while pretending to be the router.

A deauth attack floods a target device with these frames, causing it to disconnect from the network repeatedly. In security research, this is used to test whether a network's monitoring systems (IDS/IPS) detect and alert on such attacks.

How to Run a Deauth (on Your Own Network)

1

Scan for APs First

Run "Scan APs" to populate the AP list with your own router. Note its number in the list.

2

Select Deauth Target

In Marauder menu → Deauth → Select AP → choose your target AP from the scan list.

3

Start Attack

Select Deauth All Clients or a specific client. The attack runs continuously until you stop it.

4

Stop and Analyze

Press back or OK to stop. Observe how long devices took to disconnect and whether they reconnected automatically.

💡
PMF / WPA3 Networks Are Protected

If the target AP uses WPA3 or WPA2 with PMF (Protected Management Frames / 802.11w), deauth attacks will not work. This is the expected and correct behavior — PMF was specifically designed to defend against these attacks. A "failed" deauth on a PMF network actually confirms the network is properly secured.

6.2 — Evil Portal

Evil Portal creates a rogue Wi-Fi access point that presents a fake login/captive portal page when users connect. In authorized security testing, this is used to simulate phishing attacks and test whether employees can identify fake login pages.

  • In Marauder: Evil Portal → select a portal template or create custom HTML
  • The board broadcasts a new SSID — any device that connects is shown the portal page
  • Credentials or form entries can be captured and logged
🔴
Extremely High Legal Risk

Evil Portal is one of the most legally sensitive tools on this board. Running it in public spaces or on networks without authorization is a serious crime in virtually every jurisdiction. Only use in controlled, authorized environments with explicit written consent from all parties involved.

6.3 — Beacon Flood

Beacon flood broadcasts dozens of fake SSID names into the surrounding wireless environment. Real networks disappear among the flood of phantom networks. Used in security research to:

  • Test Wi-Fi scanner resilience and IDS detection thresholds
  • Study how client devices behave when exposed to massive SSID lists
  • Assess RF environment impact on network operations

In Marauder: Beacon → Rickroll (built-in fun SSID list) or Custom (define your own SSIDs) → Start.

6.4 — WPA Handshake Capture (Sniff)

The board can passively sniff the network for EAPOL packets — these are the handshake messages exchanged when a device connects to a WPA2 network. Capturing an EAPOL/PMKID handshake is the first step in offline WPA2 password auditing.

In Marauder: Sniff → EAPOL → the board logs any WPA handshakes it captures to the Flipper Zero display/log.

This is useful for authorized network security auditing — verifying whether a WPA2 password meets complexity requirements.

Firmware Guide

Understanding the pre-loaded firmware and compatible frameworks

What's Pre-Loaded: Marauder 5G

The board ships with Marauder 5G firmware pre-flashed onto the RTL8720DN chip. This is a port of the popular Wi-Fi Marauder project adapted for the RTL8720DN's dual-band 5 GHz capability. You don't need to flash anything to the board to get started.

Marauder 5G features included out of the box:

  • scanap — scan for access points on both bands
  • scansta — scan for connected stations/clients
  • deauth — deauthentication attack
  • beacon — beacon flood with custom or preset SSIDs
  • sniff — passive packet sniffing (EAPOL, probe, PMKID)
  • evilportal — rogue AP with captive portal

Momentum Firmware (Recommended)

  • What it is: Community-maintained Flipper Zero firmware with extensive feature set
  • Key benefit: Full WiFi Marauder integration, active development, large community
  • Get it at: github.com/Next-Flip/Momentum-Firmware
  • App path for this board: Apps → GPIO → [ESP32] WiFi Marauder
  • GPIO pin setting required: TX=15, RX=16 (see Chapter 5)

Unleashed Firmware

  • What it is: Flipper Zero firmware fork focused on removing regional frequency restrictions
  • Key benefit: Allows Sub-GHz transmission on all frequencies (use responsibly and per local law)
  • Also supports: WiFi Marauder integration for this board
  • Get it at: github.com/DarkFlippers/unleashed-firmware
💡
Which Should I Choose?

For most beginners focused on Wi-Fi research with this board, Momentum is recommended — it has better documentation, more frequent updates, and a more active community. Choose Unleashed if you also need Sub-GHz frequency flexibility for other projects.

Should You Update the Board's Firmware?

The Marauder 5G firmware on the board itself (not Flipper firmware — the firmware on the RTL8720DN chip) generally does not need to be updated for everyday use. The pre-loaded version works reliably with both Momentum and Unleashed.

Only update the board firmware if:

  • A new Marauder 5G release fixes a specific bug you are encountering
  • You want to access a new feature added in a later release
  • Instructed to update by official SDRLab support

Custom Development

Building and flashing custom firmware with PlatformIO

⚠️
Advanced Users Only

This chapter is for users with programming experience. If you're a beginner, the pre-loaded Marauder 5G firmware is all you need. You can safely skip this chapter and return when ready.

What is PlatformIO?

PlatformIO is a cross-platform development environment (IDE extension for VSCode) that supports hundreds of microcontroller boards. The RTL8720DN/BW16 is supported via the Ameba D Arduino framework, which means you can write Arduino-style C++ code for this board.

Setup

  1. Install Visual Studio Code
  2. Install the PlatformIO IDE extension from the VSCode marketplace
  3. Create a new project: Platform = Realtek RTL8720DN (BW16), Framework = Arduino
  4. PlatformIO will download the Ameba D board package automatically

Flashing Custom Firmware

The RTL8720DN is flashed via UART (serial), not USB directly. You need a USB-to-serial adapter (CP2102 or CH340 recommended).

platformio.ini — BW16 Configuration
[env:bw16] platform = realtek-ambz2 board = bw16 framework = arduino upload_port = /dev/ttyUSB0 ; Windows: COM3 or similar upload_speed = 1500000

Entering Bootloader Mode for Flashing

1

Disconnect from Flipper Zero

Remove the board from Flipper Zero. You'll be powering it via the serial adapter.

2

Connect UART Adapter

Wire: TX (adapter) → RX (board) | RX (adapter) → TX (board) | GND → GND | 3.3V → 3.3V

3

Enter Bootloader

Hold the BOOT button on the board, then plug in USB to the serial adapter, then release the button.

4

Upload from PlatformIO

In VSCode, click the Upload arrow in the PlatformIO toolbar. The flash process takes 30–60 seconds.

Troubleshooting

Common problems, their causes, and how to fix them

Problem Likely Cause Solution
Board not detected / "Board not found" in app Wrong GPIO pin settings OR stock firmware Verify firmware is Momentum/Unleashed. Set ESP32 pin = 15, 16 in GPIO Pin Settings. Re-seat the board.
No 5 GHz networks appear in scan Antenna not attached (U.FL revision), or local 5 GHz band is DFS-restricted Attach antenna if U.FL board. Note: DFS channels (52–144) require radar detection — the board may skip these by default. Try scanning in a location with 5 GHz APs.
Flipper Zero battery drains significantly faster Normal — the board draws 150–250 mA from GPIO during active use This is expected behavior. Keep sessions shorter if battery is a concern. The board does not have its own battery.
Deauth attack has no effect Target AP uses 802.11w/PMF (Protected Management Frames) or WPA3 Expected behavior — PMF protects against deauth. The network is properly secured. Try on an older WPA2-only network for testing.
Board gets warm during use Normal operating temperature for RTL8720DN under Wi-Fi load Normal. The chip is rated −40°C to 85°C. Warmth during active scanning is expected. If the board becomes uncomfortably hot to touch, stop use and check for short circuits.
WiFi Marauder app not found in menu App not installed in your firmware build In Momentum: go to Apps → Firmware Apps catalog → install WiFi Marauder. In Unleashed: install via the Flipper App Store method for that firmware.
Flipper Zero freezes on boot after attaching board Board not fully seated, wrong orientation, or damaged pin Power off completely. Remove board. Inspect GPIO pins for bends. Re-seat carefully, ensuring full alignment before pressing down.
Scan shows very few networks PCB antenna version — limited range, or environment has few APs Move to a location with known Wi-Fi activity. If U.FL version, check antenna is fully connected. Try both 2.4GHz and 5GHz scans separately.

Beginner Glossary

SSID
Service Set Identifier — the human-readable name of a Wi-Fi network (e.g. "HomeWifi"). What you see when browsing for networks.
BSSID
Basic Service Set Identifier — the MAC (hardware) address of a Wi-Fi access point. Unique to each router/AP hardware.
RSSI
Received Signal Strength Indicator — signal power in dBm. More negative = weaker. −30 excellent, −70 usable, −90 very weak.
Deauth
Deauthentication — a Wi-Fi management frame that forces a client to disconnect from an access point. Used in security research to test resilience.
PMF / 802.11w
Protected Management Frames — Wi-Fi security feature that encrypts and authenticates management frames, preventing deauth attacks.
EAPOL
Extensible Authentication Protocol over LAN — the "handshake" frames exchanged when a device connects to a WPA2 network. Used in security auditing.
GPIO
General Purpose Input/Output — the programmable pins on Flipper Zero's header used to connect expansion boards like this one.
BLE
Bluetooth Low Energy — a power-efficient Bluetooth standard used by IoT devices, wearables, and peripherals. BLE 5.0 has ~4× the range of BLE 4.x.
U.FL / IPEX
A tiny coaxial antenna connector used on small PCBs. Allows attaching an external antenna for extended Wi-Fi range.
DFS Channel
Dynamic Frequency Selection — 5 GHz channels (52–144) that require radar detection to avoid interference with aviation/weather radar. Some may be restricted by regulation.

Safety, Legal & Responsible Use

Understanding the legal boundaries of Wi-Fi security research

🔴
Read This Before Using Any Active Feature

This board is a security research tool, not a consumer product. Every active feature (deauth, beacon flood, evil portal, sniffing) has significant legal implications. Misuse is a criminal offense in most countries, not merely a civil matter.

What IS Allowed

  • Your own home network — any testing on a network you own and control
  • Authorized penetration tests — with explicit written authorization from the network owner
  • Educational lab environments — dedicated isolated test networks (e.g. a travel router purchased specifically for testing)
  • Your own devices — testing against hardware you own in an isolated environment
  • Passive scanning — in most jurisdictions, passively receiving/analyzing publicly broadcast beacon frames is generally permitted, but verify local laws

What Is NOT Allowed

  • Deauthenticating networks you don't own — this is a denial-of-service attack and is illegal
  • Evil Portal on public Wi-Fi — this is a criminal phishing/fraud act
  • Testing neighbor's networks — even if you can reach their signal
  • Corporate networks without written authorization — even for "educational purposes"
  • Transmitting on restricted 5 GHz DFS channels without proper authorization
  • Any use intended to intercept, modify, or disrupt communications you are not authorized to access

Applicable Laws (Summary)

  • Taiwan (Republic of China) — Communications Protection Act (通訊保障及監察法), Criminal Code §315-1 (violation of privacy)
  • United States — Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA)
  • European Union — Directive 2013/40/EU on attacks against information systems; national implementations
  • Japan — Unauthorized Computer Access Law (不正アクセス行為の禁止等に関する法律)
  • Most jurisdictions — unauthorized network interference can be prosecuted under computer crime, wiretapping, or electronic fraud laws

Getting Started Responsibly

The safest way to learn with this board:

  1. Buy a cheap travel router (TP-Link TL-WR802N, ~$20) — use it as your dedicated test AP. You own it completely.
  2. Create isolated test clients — connect an old phone or laptop to the test router. Now you have a fully controlled environment.
  3. Practice all features on this isolated setup — no legal risk, full learning.
  4. Enroll in a certified ethical hacking course if you want to advance to professional network security work.
